A step-by-step guide · about 30–45 minutes

Let's fix your Zoom calls

Your internet is fast. Your speed test showed about 250 Mbps, and a Zoom video call needs less than 4. So the problem is almost certainly not your AT&T plan. It's usually the short trip between your laptop and your Wi-Fi box. This guide walks you through the fixes, easiest first.

You don't need to be technical for this.

Each step tells you exactly what to click. Tick the box at the bottom of each step when you've done it. This page remembers your ticks on this computer, so you can stop and come back.

Why this happens

Fast isn't the same as steady

A speed test is like measuring how wide a road is. Zoom cares about whether the road has potholes. A video call sends tiny bits of sound and picture every split second. If even a few bits arrive late or get lost, your voice cuts out or the picture freezes, even on a very fast connection.

The most common causes, roughly in order: being too far from the Wi-Fi box (or having walls, floors or appliances in the way), the router needing a restart, the laptop saving battery by weakening its Wi-Fi, and other things using the internet at the same time.

Do this before every important call

The 2-minute pre-call checklist

Even after everything is fixed, these habits keep calls reliable.

The fixes

Work through these in order

Do one step, then try a Zoom call (or the free test call at zoom.us/test). If the problem is gone, you can stop. If not, move to the next step.

  1. 1

    Restart the AT&T Wi-Fi box

    5 minutes · easy

    Routers run all day, every day, and slowly get "tired." A restart clears that out.

    1. Find the AT&T box (called a gateway). It's usually black with a few small lights on the front.
    2. Unplug its power cord from the wall or from the back of the box.
    3. Wait a full 60 seconds.
    4. Plug it back in. Wait about 5 minutes until the lights stop blinking and stay steady.
    5. Restart your laptop too.

    Don't press any tiny "Reset" hole with a pin. That erases the Wi-Fi name and password. Unplugging is all you need.

    If a light is red after 10 minutes, call AT&T (number at the bottom of this page).

  2. 2

    Plug in the charger and turn off battery saving

    2 minutes · easy

    On battery, Windows quietly turns down power to the Wi-Fi to make the battery last. That alone can cause dropouts.

    1. Plug in the charger.
    2. Click the Start button (the Windows logo at the bottom of the screen), then click Settings (the gear).
    3. Click System, then Power & battery.
    4. Find Power mode and choose Best performance.
    5. If you see Energy saver (or Battery saver), make sure it's Off.

    Your screen may look a little different. Look for the same words.

  3. 3

    Check your Wi-Fi signal where you work

    5 minutes · easy

    Look at the Wi-Fi symbol at the bottom-right of the screen, next to the clock. The more curved bars that are filled in, the stronger your signal.

    • All bars filled: great.
    • Only 1 or 2 bars: this is very likely your problem. Go to Step 4.

    Try this test: carry the laptop into the same room as the AT&T box and join the Zoom test call at zoom.us/test. If it works well there but badly at your desk, distance or walls are the cause.

  4. 4

    Help the Wi-Fi reach you

    10–15 minutes · easy

    Wi-Fi is like a lamp's light: it gets dimmer through walls and floors, and some things block it badly.

    • Move the AT&T box out in the open, up high (on a shelf, not the floor), and not inside a cabinet, closet or behind the TV.
    • Keep it away from microwaves, cordless phone bases, fish tanks, mirrors and large metal things like filing cabinets.
    • Or move your work spot closer, ideally in the same room or one wall away.

    The AT&T box can only move as far as its cables reach. If you can't move it or your desk, Step 8 (a cable) or Step 9 (a mesh system) will solve it.

  5. 5

    Update Zoom and lighten its load

    10 minutes · easy

    Update Zoom: open the Zoom app, click your picture or initials in the top-right corner, then click Check for updates. Install anything it finds.

    Make video lighter: click your picture → Settings → Video (or "Video & effects"):

    • Untick HD. People will still see you clearly.
    • If you use a virtual background or blur, try turning it to None. These work the laptop hard.

    During a bad call: turning your camera off for a minute often brings the sound right back.

    If your work requires a VPN (a program that connects you to the office network), ask IT whether Zoom should go through it. A VPN often causes exactly these problems.

  6. 6

    Update the laptop's Wi-Fi software

    15–30 minutes · mostly waiting

    Dell laptops come with an app that installs fixes for the Wi-Fi and other parts. Out-of-date Wi-Fi software is a common cause of dropouts on newer laptops.

    1. Click Start and type SupportAssist (or Dell Command Update). Open it.
    2. Click Check for updates (sometimes called "Get drivers & downloads"). Install everything it offers.
    3. Next, open Settings → Windows Update → Check for updates. Install updates, then click Advanced options → Optional updates and install any listed under "Driver updates."
    4. Restart the laptop when asked.

    Do this after work hours. Updates can take a while and need a restart.

  7. 7

    Move to a quieter Wi-Fi channel

    20 minutes · a little more involved

    Your Wi-Fi talks on a "channel," like a lane on a highway. If several neighbors' Wi-Fi uses the same lane, everyone gets in each other's way. This happens a lot in apartments, condos and close-together homes.

    Part A: see which channels your neighbors use

    1. Click Start, type Microsoft Store and open it.
    2. Search for WiFi Analyzer and install the free one (a blue icon). Open it at your desk.
    3. Open the Analyze tab. Each hump is a nearby Wi-Fi network. Look for the channel with the fewest and smallest humps. Check both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz.

    Part B: change your channel

    1. Find the Device Access Code on the sticker on the side or bottom of the AT&T box. Write it down.
    2. On the laptop, open your web browser, click the address bar at the very top, type 192.168.1.254 and press Enter.
    3. Click Home Network, then Wi-Fi, then Advanced Options. Type the Device Access Code when asked.
    4. Before changing anything, take a photo of the screen with your phone. That way you can always put it back.
    5. Under 2.4 GHz, change Channel from Automatic to whichever of 1, 6 or 11 was least crowded. Only use those three.
    6. Under 5 GHz, pick the least crowded of 36, 40, 44, 48, 149, 153, 157 or 161.
    7. Scroll down and click Save. Wi-Fi will drop for a minute and come back.

    Do this outside work hours. If anything goes wrong, set both channels back to Automatic (using your photo) or restart the box as in Step 1. Your Wi-Fi name and password won't change.

    The screens may use slightly different words depending on your AT&T box model. If you'd rather not do this part, your helper can walk you through it on the phone.

  8. 8

    The most reliable fix: use a cable

    a one-time purchase · about $25

    Best fix for work calls

    A cable from the AT&T box to your laptop skips Wi-Fi completely. For anyone whose job depends on video calls, this is the gold standard. It usually fixes freezing and dropouts for good.

    1. Look at the sides of your laptop for a wide, squarish socket (like a phone jack, but bigger). Many newer Dells don't have one, and that's fine.
    2. If not, buy a "USB-C to Ethernet adapter" (about $15–25 at Best Buy, Staples, Walmart or Amazon). It plugs into the small oval USB-C port on the laptop.
    3. Buy an Ethernet cable long enough to reach your desk ("Cat 6", in a length like 25 or 50 feet).
    4. Plug one end into any yellow or numbered port on the back of the AT&T box, the other into the adapter.

    That's it. No settings needed. The laptop automatically uses the cable. If a cable can't reach your desk, Step 9 is the next-best option.

  9. 9

    Add a mesh Wi-Fi system

    a one-time purchase · about $80–200

    If a cable won't reach and your Wi-Fi is weak at your desk, a mesh system helps. It's a set of 2 or 3 small boxes that work together like a relay team, carrying strong Wi-Fi to every room.

    Good, affordable options (prices are rough and change often):

    Simplest

    AT&T Smart Wi-Fi Extender

    Made to work with your AT&T box. Set up in the AT&T Smart Home Manager app. Ask AT&T about price; it's sometimes discounted or included.

    Best value

    TP-Link Deco (Wi-Fi 6), 2-pack

    Models like the Deco X20 or X55. Easy phone-app setup. Often about $80–150.

    Easiest app

    Amazon eero 6+, 2- or 3-pack

    Very simple app, good support. Often about $130–200, cheaper on sale.

    • Look for "Wi-Fi 6" (or 6E or 7) on the box. Skip anything labelled only "Wi-Fi 5" or "AC".
    • A 2-pack covers most homes. Choose a 3-pack for a large or multi-floor home.
    • Put the second box about halfway between the AT&T box and your desk, out in the open. Not in your office itself.

    Ask your helper before you buy, or buy from a store with easy returns. Your helper can do the setup over the phone. There are a couple of settings on the AT&T box that work best when set up together.

    Avoid the cheap plug-in "Wi-Fi booster" gadgets. They often make video calls worse, not better.

Find out if it's working

Check your connection's "steadiness" numbers

Zoom can show you how steady your connection is. Do this at your normal desk, ideally while on a call or the test call at zoom.us/test.

  1. In the Zoom app, click your picture or initials (top-right) → Settings.
  2. Click Statistics on the left, then the Network (or Overall) tab.
  3. Write down the three numbers below.
NumberWhat it meansGoodOkayProblem
LatencyHow long a bit of sound takes to arriveunder 100 ms100–150over 150
JitterHow uneven the arrival times areunder 30 ms30–40over 40
Packet lossHow much sound and video gets lost0–1%1–2%over 2%

These are general rules of thumb, not official Zoom limits. "ms" means milliseconds (thousandths of a second).

One more free test (optional)

Go to waveform.com/tools/bufferbloat and click Start Test. It gives a letter grade. An A or B is good. A C, D or F means the connection gets "jammed up" when it's busy, which causes exactly the freezing you're seeing.

Still having trouble?

Who to call

AT&T Internet support: 1-800-288-2020

Tell them: "My speed test is fine, but I'm getting freezing and dropped video calls over Wi-Fi. Can you check my gateway's Wi-Fi and the line for errors?" You can also use the free AT&T Smart Home Manager app on your phone to restart the box and see your Wi-Fi strength.

Your work's IT or help desk: if you use a VPN or a work-managed laptop, they can check whether their software is slowing Zoom down. Showing them your numbers from the table above helps a lot.

For the person helping

Technical notes

What to look at in her results
  • Good near the gateway, bad at the desk: RF coverage. Wired Ethernet via USB-C adapter, or an AT&T Smart Wi-Fi Extender / mesh node in wired-backhaul mode if possible.
  • Bad everywhere, even wired: line or gateway issue (ask AT&T to check ONT/gateway logs), or VPN / endpoint security software on the laptop.
  • Bufferbloat C or worse: upstream saturation from other devices (cloud backups, camera uploads, game downloads). AT&T gateways have no SQM; a separate router with SQM/Smart Queue behind the gateway in IP Passthrough mode fixes it.
  • Packet loss spikes but low latency: classic Wi-Fi retransmit or roaming issue. Check the adapter's band.
Laptop-side checks (Windows 11)
  • Wi-Fi adapter power saving: Device Manager → Network adapters → the Intel/Killer/Realtek/Qualcomm Wi-Fi adapter → Power Management → untick "Allow the computer to turn off this device to save power." Under Advanced, set Roaming Aggressiveness to Lowest if present.
  • Which band: Settings → Network & internet → Wi-Fi → (network) properties shows Network band. 5 GHz (or 6 GHz) is what you want; 2.4 GHz at the desk is a red flag.
  • Band steering: BGW-series gateways merge 2.4/5 GHz under one SSID. If the laptop keeps falling back to 2.4 GHz, split the bands at 192.168.1.254 → Home Network → Wi-Fi (Advanced Options) and connect the laptop to the 5 GHz SSID only. Device access code is on the gateway's sticker.
  • Killer Wi-Fi cards (some Dells): the Killer Control Center / Prioritization Engine has a history of causing call jitter. Use Dell's current driver and disable prioritization, or install the Intel driver-only package.
  • Background traffic: OneDrive sync, Windows Update delivery optimization, backup agents. Pause OneDrive during calls; Settings → Windows Update → Advanced → Delivery Optimization → off.
  • Last resort: Settings → Network & internet → Advanced network settings → Network reset (she'll need to rejoin Wi-Fi afterwards).
Gateway-side checks
  • At 192.168.1.254: Broadband → Status for line errors; Home Network → Wi-Fi for channel. Avoid auto-picking a crowded channel; on 5 GHz prefer 80 MHz on a non-DFS channel (36–48 or 149–161) if DFS events are dropping clients.
  • Count connected devices in Smart Home Manager. Older 2.4 GHz-only devices can drag airtime down.
  • If the gateway is several years old or a BGW210, ask AT&T about a swap to a current model.
  • Channel choice: 2.4 GHz only 1/6/11 (overlap otherwise); 5 GHz non-DFS 36–48 or 149–161 avoids radar-triggered channel switches that drop clients for ~1 min. Keep 5 GHz at 80 MHz unless the area is very dense, then try 40 MHz. If Automatic picks well after a reboot, leaving it there is fine.
  • Mesh setup: to avoid double NAT, either run the mesh in bridge/access-point mode (eero: "Bridge mode"; Deco: "Access Point mode") behind the gateway, or keep the mesh in router mode and set the gateway to IP Passthrough (Firewall → IP Passthrough, DHCPS-fixed to the mesh MAC). Then turn off the gateway's own Wi-Fi radios so devices don't cling to it. Wired backhaul between mesh nodes beats wireless if there's any way to run a cable.
